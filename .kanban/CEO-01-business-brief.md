# iHealth Pharmacy — Website Upgrade CEO Brief

> Prepared by: Business Owner / CEO proxy
> Project: Upgrade the Friendly demo into a professional, agency-grade pharmacy website
> Live demo reference: https://dhr232.github.io/ihealth-pharmacy-designs/variants/friendly
> Brand anchor: Logo red #C01D16. Current palette uses green #16a34a and cream #fffdf8.

---

## Business positioning

### Audience
- Local families in Abbotsford, BC who want a pharmacy they can walk into and talk to.
- Seniors managing multiple prescriptions and preferring in-person advice.
- Parents filling kids’ prescriptions, asking about vaccines, or looking for trusted health basics.
- Small-business employees and walk-in customers who need same-day service without a big-box experience.
- Newcomers searching online for “pharmacy near me” in Abbotsford.

### Core promise in one sentence
“You get a pharmacist who knows your name, gives you time, and makes health advice feel simple.”

### What makes us different from big-box pharmacies
- Independent and locally owned: decisions are made here, not by a regional head office.
- Pharmacists available for real conversations; no rushing you through a conveyor belt.
- Same-day prescription fills and direct follow-up calls.
- We stock what our neighbourhood actually asks for, not just national SKUs.
- Community connection: local events, school sponsorships, health screenings, and word-of-mouth trust.

---

## Must-have credibility signals

1. **Active pharmacy license and credentials** — list the pharmacy license number, accredited pharmacist team, and relevant affiliations (e.g., BC Pharmacy Association, College of Pharmacists of BC).
2. **Local address with map + hours** — full street address in Abbotsford, real hours, phone number, and a visible map. No PO boxes or vague “Lower Mainland” language.
3. **Years in business / experience** — a clear statement of how long we have served the community; if new, emphasize the founders’ combined years of pharmacy experience.
4. **Services with specifics** — prescription transfers, medication reviews, flu shots, travel vaccines, compounding, blister packaging, free delivery zone, and health consultations. Avoid empty labels like “Wellness Services.”
5. **Real patient proof** — 2–4 authentic-sounding testimonials with first names and local context. Bonus: a visible rating or review source (Google, Facebook) if available.
6. **Professional imagery** — actual pharmacy interior, staff in clean branded attire (or high-quality, non-stock-looking photography), and a clean logo lockup. No obvious AI-generated faces or fake clinics.
7. **Clear contact and emergency guidance** — phone number is click-to-call, hours are prominent, and there is a sentence directing emergencies to 911 or the nearest ER. Builds responsibility and trust.

---

## Tone of voice

- **Professional but warm.** Competent enough to handle your medication; human enough to remember your kid’s name.
- **Plain English first.** Use words a 70-year-old grandmother and a busy parent both understand. “Medication review” not “comprehensive pharmaceutical care optimization.”
- **Confident, not boastful.** Say what we do and why it matters; skip superlatives like “the best pharmacy in Canada.”
- **Local, not generic.** Reference Abbotsford, neighbourhood routines, and real community needs.
- **Respectful of health concerns.** Never fear-monger. Never make medical guarantees. Use calm, helpful language.

---

## What to avoid

1. **Emojis in headings or buttons** — no pills, hearts, or sparkles in the UI copy. They make us look like a social media page, not a health provider.
2. **More than three core colors** — red, green, and cream is already busy. Keep it disciplined. No rainbow accents, gradients everywhere, or neon call-to-action buttons.
3. **Cartoon shadows, bouncy animations, or playful illustrations** — the Friendly variant leaned warm, but professional means restrained. Flat, subtle shadows only.
4. **Vague, templated copy** — no “Your health is our priority” or “We care about you” without backing it up with services, hours, or credentials.
5. **Missing or broken contact details** — an unclickable phone number, generic email, or placeholder address instantly kills trust.
6. **Fake testimonials** — do not invent full names, photos, or five-star stories. If we only have one real review, use that one honestly.
7. **Auto-playing music or loud popups** — no notifications begging for permission, no newsletter gates blocking the phone number.
8. **Wall of text** — keep paragraphs short. Use headings, bullets, and clear calls to action. Dense blocks feel institutional.

---

## Approval workflow

1. **Internal review** — UI engineer, frontend developer, and content reviewer check the upgraded site against this brief, run it on mobile and desktop, and confirm all credibility signals are present.
2. **Client preview link** — share a clean staging URL with the pharmacy owner. Owner reviews for accuracy (hours, address, services, team names, license details). Feedback is collected in writing.
3. **Final sign-off** — owner approves the final version in writing. No launch until sign-off is captured. Only then deploy to production.

---

## Key decisions for the team

1. **Hero layout and primary call to action** — decide whether the homepage hero leads with “Call us,” “Transfer your prescription,” or “Find us.” Document the choice and why it matches the main customer path.
2. **Photography decision** — choose between using real staff/store photos, booking a quick photo shoot, or selecting a tight set of premium, non-stock lifestyle images. Document the source and licensing status.
3. **Service page structure** — decide whether services live on one scannable page with anchors or on separate pages for prescriptions, vaccinations, and clinical services. Document the information architecture and URL plan.
